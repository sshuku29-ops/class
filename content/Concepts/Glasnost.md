# Glasnost / Гласность 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[13 Little Vera]] on the prof's exam study sheet (paired with [[Perestroika]]). Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

**Mikhail Gorbachev**'s policy of openness, formalized 1986 within the broader [[Perestroika]] reform program. Discussed in the Little Vera lecture and in **Birgit Beumers**, *A History of Russian Cinema* (Berg, 2009).

## Definition

Glasnost (гласность, "publicity" or "openness") is Gorbachev's policy of relaxing Soviet censorship and permitting public exposure of the system's failures. Key components: removal of bans on previously suppressed authors (Solzhenitsyn, Bulgakov, Pasternak); release of shelved films (most famously Tengiz Abuladze's *Repentance*, 1984/1986; Aleksandr Askoldov's *Commissar*, 1967/1987; Muratova's *Brief Encounters*, 1968/1986); permission for explicit public criticism of Stalinism, the [[Brezhnev Stagnation]], and the contemporary Soviet system; freer press and public debate.

## Key context

Glasnost is the institutional precondition for [[Chernukha]] cinema and for the late-Soviet documentary explosion. The opening also forces a public reckoning with the Stalinist past that had been only partially admitted under Khrushchev — the [[Cinematic Deconstruction of Stalin]] tradition (*Repentance* 1986, *I Served as Stalin's Bodyguard* 1989, *Inner Circle* 1990, [[14 Burnt by the Sun]] 1994) is glasnost's long shadow. Glasnost also enables [[Sexual Desublimation]] in Soviet cinema; *Little Vera* (1987) and the subsequent chernukha wave depict erotic and domestic content that had been forbidden across all prior Soviet periods.

## Films using it

- 🎯 [[13 Little Vera]] (1987) — produced under glasnost
- All late-1980s releases of previously banned films

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

*Brief Encounters* (Muratova, 1968) sat shelved for 18 years; glasnost in 1986 finally allowed its release. The same opening permits *Little Vera* (1987) to reach Soviet audiences with content (explicit sex, alcoholism, family violence) that would have been impossible to release at any earlier moment. *Little Vera*'s opening sequence — Vera in her cramped industrial-port-town apartment, the Pushkin epigraph "[[Cherish Your Youthful Innocence]]" deployed ironically against the chernukha that follows — is glasnost's representational logic at the level of mise-en-scène: the camera shows what the system had previously varnished.
