# Modal Schizophrenia 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[06 Chapaev]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

**Katerina Clark**, *The Soviet Novel: History as Ritual* (University of Chicago Press, 1981), Chapter 2 "Socialist Realism with Shores: The Convention for the Positive Hero." The conceptual root is Anatolii Lunacharsky's definition of socialist realism — see [[Lunacharsky's SR Definition]].

## Definition

Modal Schizophrenia is Clark's term for Socialist Realism's defining representational logic: the simultaneous depiction of "what is" and "what ought to be" in a single fused mode. Lunacharsky's formulation: the SR artist "does not accept reality as reality is. He accepts it as it will be." The "schizophrenia" is structural — the work must show present Soviet life and future Soviet utopia as if they were the same thing. Realism (the present) and utopia (the future) coexist in the same diegetic frame.

## Key context

Modal Schizophrenia is the formal solution to the contradiction at the heart of Stalinist culture: state propaganda demanded representations of full revolutionary success at the precise moment Soviet citizens were experiencing rationing, famine, terror, and forced labor. The SR convention required films and novels to fuse "is" with "ought" so seamlessly that the audience experienced future-utopia as present-reality. This is the deep cultural function of Stalinist musical comedy ([[07 Volga-Volga]]) and Stalinist epic ([[08 The Fall of Berlin]]): they teach Soviet citizens to see their reality through Modal Schizophrenia's double exposure.

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

In *Volga-Volga* (1938), Aleksandrov's Moscow shines with utopian abundance — gleaming Stalinist architecture, joyful citizens, the Magic Tablecloth promise of consumer goods — at the precise moment the historical 1938 Moscow was running on rationing, the [[Great Terror]] was executing thousands per day, and ordinary citizens were being denounced and disappeared. Modal Schizophrenia is the formal mechanism: the camera shows Moscow "as it will be" (the utopian socialist future) while the title cards and dialogue insist this is "as it is" (the contemporary present). The film teaches Soviet viewers to read their actual reality through this future-tense lens.
