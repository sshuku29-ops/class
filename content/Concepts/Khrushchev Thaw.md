# Khrushchev Thaw 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[09 The Cranes Are Flying]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate. **The prof asks specifically for years AND important events/developments — drill the dates.**

## Source

Term named after **Ilya Ehrenburg**'s novella *The Thaw* (Оттепель, 1954). Theorized for Soviet cinema by **Alexander Prokhorov**, "Springtime for Soviet Cinema: Re/Viewing the 1960s" (KinoKultura special issue / monograph). Discussed at length in **Lynne Attwood**, *The New Soviet Man and Woman* (1990).

## Definition

The Khrushchev Thaw is the period of post-Stalin liberalization in Soviet cultural and political life, conventionally dated **February 1956 to August 1968**. It begins with Khrushchev's [[Khrushchev Secret Speech]] denouncing Stalin's crimes at the [[20th Party Congress]]; it ends with the Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia that crushes the Prague Spring. Major developments include: mass release of GULAG prisoners (rehabilitation), relaxation of censorship, expansion of foreign cultural exchange, the [[Soviet New Wave]] in cinema, the publication of Solzhenitsyn's *One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich* (1962), the housing program (khrushchevki), and the [[1955 Abortion Re-legalized]] decree.

## Key context

The Thaw permits in cinema what Stalinism repressed: female subjectivity ([[Femininity]]), wartime trauma ([[Trench Truth]]), individual interiority, formal experimentation. The generation produced by these changes — the [[Shestidesyatniki]], "those of the sixties" — becomes the cultural-political backbone of late-Soviet dissent and post-Soviet liberal politics. The Thaw is not a full break with Stalinism (the [[Big Soviet Family]] structure persists in modified form) but a real loosening that produces the conditions for the [[Soviet New Wave]] and for Veronika in *Cranes Are Flying* (1957) as the first major Soviet heroine permitted to be flawed, individual, traumatized.

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

*The Cranes Are Flying* (1957), released within months of [[Khrushchev Secret Speech|Khrushchev's Secret Speech]] and the Thaw's official opening, embodies the period's representational permission. Veronika, the heroine, is the first major Soviet female protagonist allowed to be flawed (she fails to wait for Boris), traumatized (the Mark assault), and individually subjective (Urusevsky's subjective camera). The Thaw is the precondition for Veronika's [[Femininity]] — Stalinist cinema would have been forbidden to film her this way. The Thaw's end in 1968 (Czechoslovakia invasion) is also Muratova's *Brief Encounters* shelving — the same year, the same political shift.
