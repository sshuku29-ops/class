# Kulak / Кулак 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[04 Earth]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

Russian socio-political category dating to the late Imperial period; weaponized by Lenin and then Stalin as a class enemy designation. Term and policy framework discussed in the Earth lecture and the assigned NEP / collectivization readings.

## Definition

A kulak (literally "fist") was originally a designation for the more prosperous peasant of pre-revolutionary Russia — one who owned more land, hired labor, or lent money. Under Lenin, kulaks were classed as the rural bourgeoisie, an internal enemy of the revolution. Under Stalin, "kulak" became an open political category applied at the discretion of local officials during forced collectivization — anyone resisting the collectivization of farmland could be labeled a kulak.

## Key context

Stalin's "Socialist Offensive" against the kulaks (announced 1929) marked the official end of [[NEP]] and the beginning of forced collectivization. **Dekulakization** (раскулачивание, 1929–1933) deported, imprisoned, or executed an estimated several million peasants labeled as kulaks. The campaign caused mass famine, including the Holodomor in Ukraine (1932–33), which killed an estimated 3.5–7.5 million Ukrainians. Dovzhenko's *Earth* (1930) presents collectivization affirmatively, with the kulak as Christ-killer figure murdering the Komsomol hero — but the actual historical reality of mass starvation and deportation is offscreen.

## Films using it

- 🎯 [[04 Earth]] (1930) — the kulak Khoma murders the Komsomol leader Vasyl

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

In *Earth* (1930), the kulak Khoma murders Vasyl, the young Bolshevik who has driven the village's first collective tractor. Dovzhenko stages Vasyl's death as a mythic-religious moment ([[Monumental Tableau]]) — Vasyl dances joyfully under the moon and is shot, becoming a martyr of the collectivization campaign. The film's affirmative representation of the kulak-as-enemy serves the 1929 Soviet propaganda mission: the rural class war is presented as inevitable, redemptive, and biological-cyclical rather than as the policy-driven catastrophe it actually was.
