# Male Gaze 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — explicitly named on the prof's exam study sheet under the essay rubric: "try to name specific theoreticians, such as Eisenstein, Vertov, Tarkovsky, **Laura Mulvey and the Male Gaze**." Part I-B Option 1 candidate AND essential for the essay (Option 2).

## Source

**Laura Mulvey**, "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema," published in **Screen, vol. 16, no. 3 (Autumn 1975), pp. 6–18**. Foundational text of feminist film theory. Mulvey's essay draws on Freudian and Lacanian psychoanalysis but its core argument is structural and accessible without that framework.

## Definition

The Male Gaze names the structural feature of classical narrative cinema: the camera, the male protagonist, and the spectator share a single masculine viewing position; women on screen are coded as the passive object of the look, not the active bearer of it. Mulvey writes: "In a world ordered by sexual imbalance, pleasure in looking has been split between active/male and passive/female. The determining male gaze projects its fantasy onto the female figure, which is styled accordingly." Women are coded for "to-be-looked-at-ness" — their visual presence freezes the narrative drive while the male figure carries the action forward.

## Key context

Mulvey's essay is the founding text of feminist film theory. It identifies two visual pleasures the cinema institutionalizes: (1) [[Scopophilia]] — pleasure in looking, derived from voyeuristic distance, with the female body as object; (2) narcissistic identification with the on-screen male protagonist whose gaze the camera shares. Mulvey's call: revolutionary cinema must destroy this pleasure structure rather than simply replace its content. For your gender-equality essay track, Mulvey is the prof's named feminist theorist — her framework can be applied across the syllabus from Vertov to Zvyagintsev.

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

The Mark assault scene in *The Cranes Are Flying* (1957) is a textbook Mulvey scene: low-angle Medium Close-Up with Mark as a dark mass pressing in from the left, Veronika's face the only lit object — to-be-looked-at-ness against the void of male possessive presence. The camera's gaze structures Mark's possession; the subtitle contradiction ("I love you!" / "No!") makes the script-vs-image dynamic explicit. Even within the [[Khrushchev Thaw]]'s newly permitted [[Femininity]] — Veronika as flawed individual rather than Stakhanovite type — the film cannot fully escape the structural male gaze; the camera's complicity with Mark's act is the mechanism that lets the audience experience Veronika's coercion as well as register it.
