# Kino-Eye / Kinoglaz 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[03 Man with a Movie Camera]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

**Dziga Vertov**, "WE: Variant of a Manifesto" (МЫ. Вариант манифеста) in *Kinofot* 1 (1922); "Kinoks: A Revolution" (1923); "The Birth of Kino-Eye" (1924). All reprinted in **Kino-Eye: The Writings of Dziga Vertov** (ed. Annette Michelson, trans. Kevin O'Brien, 1984).

## Definition

The Kino-Eye (kinoglaz, кино-глаз) is Vertov's foundational concept: the camera as a perceptual instrument superior to the human eye. Vertov writes: "I am Kino-Eye. I am a builder. I have placed you, whom I've created today, in an extraordinary room which did not exist until just now when I also created it." The camera sees what the human eye cannot — slow motion, reverse motion, microscopic, aerial, dialectically edited — and assembles a perceptual reality the unaided human cannot directly access.

## Key context

The Kino-Eye is both a tool and an ethics. Vertov rejects fiction film (which he calls "a cine-nicotine" and "the opium of the people") in favor of documentary that captures [[Life as it is]] — life unstaged, unscripted, "caught unawares." Combined with [[Factory of Facts]] (cinema as documentary mass-production) and the [[Electric Man]] (humans-machine-fused as the new Soviet subject), the Kino-Eye is Vertov's program for a fully revolutionary cinema. Eisenstein's [[Montage of Attractions]] is the primary alternative within Soviet 1920s film theory.

## Films using it

- 🎯 [[03 Man with a Movie Camera]] (1929) — the most complete realization of the Kino-Eye program

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

In *Man with a Movie Camera* (1929), Vertov demonstrates the Kino-Eye through formal devices the human eye cannot replicate: the cameraman lies on the railroad tracks while a train passes overhead; rapid intercutting fuses the woman's blinking eye with the camera shutter focusing; a tracking shot films Moscow traffic from a moving car at impossible angles; reverse motion makes a building "construct itself." The film has no actors, no intertitles, no script — Vertov's "three no's." The Kino-Eye assembles a Soviet city the unaided human eye could never directly perceive.
