# Sotsart / Соц-арт 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[13 Little Vera]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

Late-Soviet artistic movement founded by **Vitaly Komar** and **Aleksandr Melamid** in Moscow, c. 1972 (term and movement). Theorized in **Boris Groys**, *The Total Art of Stalinism: Avant-Garde, Aesthetic Dictatorship, and Beyond* (Princeton, 1992). Discussed in the Little Vera lecture and in cultural-history readings.

## Definition

Sotsart (Соц-арт, "Socialist Art") is the late-Soviet artistic movement that ironically appropriates Socialist Realist iconography — the way Pop Art appropriated American consumer culture. Komar and Melamid emigrated to the U.S. in 1978 and exhibited in New York; their major works pastiche Stalin and Lenin imagery in the visual register of bourgeois portraiture. The movement extended into late-Soviet film and literature, where Sotsart provides a critical register for treating Soviet propaganda imagery as found material — present, drained of original meaning, available for ironic redeployment.

## Key context

Sotsart is the visual-cultural sibling of [[Chernukha]]: where chernukha shows the social reality the [[Magic Tablecloth]] hid, Sotsart shows the propaganda imagery as evidence of the system's exhaustion. Both modes are products of the late [[Brezhnev Stagnation]] cultural environment and are made publicly representable by [[Glasnost]]. Boris Groys's argument: Sotsart treats Stalinist culture not as a failed utopia but as the consummation of Russian avant-garde modernism, with its monumental imagery now available as universal cultural inheritance.

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

In *Little Vera* (1987), the Soviet visual register — slogans on the wall, May Day pageant footage, working-class kitsch decoration in Vera's apartment — is treated as Sotsart found-material: present in the frame, drained of its ideological force, evidence of the system's exhaustion rather than of its triumph. Where Stalinist visual culture deployed banners and slogans as ideological commitment, Pichul's Sotsart-adjacent mise-en-scène treats the same imagery as wallpaper texture, parodic backdrop, kitsch. This is part of [[Chernukha]]'s broader visual logic: the camera shows what the propaganda apparatus had insisted was unphotographable — its own decay.
