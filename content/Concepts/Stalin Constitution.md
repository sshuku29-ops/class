# Stalin Constitution (1936) 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[07 Volga-Volga]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

Constitution of the USSR adopted by the Eighth All-Union Congress of Soviets, **5 December 1936**. Drafted under Stalin's direct supervision; replaced the 1924 Constitution. Discussed in the Volga-Volga lecture and in **Lynne Attwood**, *The New Soviet Man and Woman: Sex-Role Socialization in the USSR* (Indiana University Press, 1990).

## Definition

The Stalin Constitution was officially proclaimed "the most democratic constitution in the world." It declared universal suffrage (replacing earlier class-based voting restrictions), formal gender equality, full social rights including the right to work, rest, education, and healthcare, and the recognition of the Soviet Union as a socialist state of workers and peasants. The Constitution was widely publicized internationally as proof of Soviet democratic credentials.

## Key context

The Stalin Constitution is the foundational document of Stalinist [[Modal Schizophrenia]]: it proclaimed full constitutional rights at the precise moment those rights were being most violently negated. The Constitution was adopted in December 1936, four months after the August 1936 Zinoviev–Kamenev show trial that opened the [[Great Terror]]. The same year (June 1936) saw the [[Anti-abortion Law 1936]], reversing NEP-era reproductive autonomy. The Constitution's promise of gender equality coexists with the cult of motherhood demanded by the anti-abortion law — the contradiction is not accidental but structural.

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

*Volga-Volga* (1938) visualizes the Stalin Constitution's promise of gender equality through the figure of Strelka, the postwoman who writes a hit song and travels to Moscow for the All-Union Music Olympiad. On paper, Strelka is the Constitution's universal-suffrage equal citizen. The film's actual cinematic logic — Strelka's song authorship dissolves into [[Isaak Dunaevsky]]'s orchestral spectacle, framed and consumed by Soviet men — registers the gap between constitutional promise and patriarchal practice. The Constitution is the textual source of Volga-Volga's [[Modal Schizophrenia]]: equality "as it will be," not as it is.
