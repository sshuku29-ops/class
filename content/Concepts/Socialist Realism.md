# Socialist Realism (SR) 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[06 Chapaev]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

Doctrine codified at the **First All-Union Congress of Soviet Writers, August 1934**, Moscow. Andrei Zhdanov's keynote address established the canonical formulation. **Katerina Clark**, *The Soviet Novel: History as Ritual* (1981) is the foundational scholarly study. The Lunacharsky formulation cited on the prof's study sheet — "does not accept reality as reality is. He accepts it as it will be" — appears in *The Film Factory: Russian and Soviet Cinema in Documents 1896–1939* (eds. Richard Taylor and Ian Christie), p. 327.

## Definition

Socialist Realism is the official aesthetic doctrine of Stalinist culture from 1934 onward, mandatory for all Soviet novels, films, plays, and visual art. Its canonical formulation: works must be **realistic in form, socialist in content**. Required features: a positive hero, a guiding elder, a journey to consciousness ([[Spontaneity-Consciousness Dialectic]]), partiinost' (party-mindedness), narodnost' (accessibility to the masses), and ideinost' (ideological purpose). The doctrine replaces the 1920s Soviet avant-garde experimentation (Vertov, Eisenstein in his more radical mode) with a unified state-mandated aesthetic.

## Key context

Socialist Realism is not just an aesthetic preference but a state-enforced cultural law. After 1934, Soviet writers, filmmakers, and artists who failed to produce SR work risked censorship, imprisonment, or execution; the [[Cineanemia]] of late Stalinism (1946–53) is partly the consequence of SR's rigidity narrowing the field of permissible production. SR's canonical underlying structure is Clark's [[Big Soviet Family]], its narrative engine is [[Spontaneity-Consciousness Dialectic]], and its representational mode is [[Modal Schizophrenia]]. SR is partially relaxed under the [[Khrushchev Thaw]] but never formally abolished.

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

*Chapaev* (1934) became the template for Soviet cinema literally weeks after the Writers' Congress codified Socialist Realism. The film hits every SR requirement: a positive hero (Chapaev), a guiding elder ([[Commissar]] Furmanov), a [[Spontaneity-Consciousness Dialectic]] (the potato-lesson scene stages it explicitly), partiinost' (the Bolshevik party as the historical agent), narodnost' (mass accessibility through clear narrative), and ideinost' (the ideological message of revolutionary consciousness). Asking whether *Volga-Volga*'s director is "a true Socialist Realist" — the prof's exam question — means asking whether Aleksandrov fulfills [[Lunacharsky's SR Definition]]: does the film show Soviet life "as it will be" rather than as it is?
