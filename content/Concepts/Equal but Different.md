# Equal but Different 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[12 Moscow Doesn't Believe in Tears]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

**Lynne Attwood**, *The New Soviet Man and Woman: Sex-Role Socialization in the USSR* (Indiana University Press, 1990), Chapter 5 "Brezhnev: Era of Stagnation." Concept also developed by **Igor Kon** in "Gender and Sexuality in Russia" — both authors are core texts per the prof's email.

## Definition

"Equal but Different" is Attwood's name for the 1970s redefinition of Soviet women's equality. Attwood's exact phrasing: "equality redefined so that it was not incompatible with women putting work in second place and devoting more time to the family." Where Khrushchev-era policy promised full equality through full employment, Brezhnev-era policy retreated: women remained formally equal AND were ideologically expected to prioritize the family. The "difference" is that women, by their nature, belong differently in the workforce than men — a softer-coded return to traditional gender hierarchy.

## Key context

"Equal but Different" is the Brezhnev-era state response to the [[Double Burden]] crisis (which Khrushchev had publicly acknowledged) AND to the [[Pro-family Campaign]] / [[Demographic Crisis]] (falling birth rates in European Soviet republics combined with rising rates in Central Asia, sharpened politically after the 1979 Afghanistan invasion). The policy answer was not redistribution of household labor but ideological reframing: women's "natural" priority is the family. Igor Kon's [[Russian Gender Duality]] (the simultaneously elevated and exploited woman) names the deeper cultural structure that "Equal but Different" articulates.

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

In *Moscow Doesn't Believe in Tears* (1979), Gosha's discovery that Katerina out-earns him triggers his masculine crisis; the film "resolves" it by showing Katerina submitting her professional success to "ordinary housewife" status. Attwood quotes Soviet film critic Neya Zorkaya on the audience response: laughter and cheers as Katerina "shut up like an ordinary housewife." Attwood's verdict on the film: "the film's ultimate message is that it is right and proper for the man to be the head of the family." This is "Equal but Different" in narrative form: Katerina is formally equal to Gosha (she earns more, holds more authority, has survived more) AND ideologically expected to defer because that is "naturally" what women want. The film makes the deferral look like fulfillment.
