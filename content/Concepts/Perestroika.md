# Perestroika / Перестройка 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[13 Little Vera]] on the prof's exam study sheet (paired with [[Glasnost]]). Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

**Mikhail Gorbachev**'s reform program, launched April 1985 (Plenum of the CPSU Central Committee). Term means "restructuring." The official policy framework is articulated in Gorbachev's book **Perestroika: New Thinking for Our Country and the World** (1987). Discussed in the Little Vera lecture and in **Lynne Attwood**, *The New Soviet Man and Woman* (1990) and **Birgit Beumers**, *A History of Russian Cinema* (Berg, 2009).

## Definition

Perestroika is the period of Gorbachev-era systemic reform of the Soviet economic and political order, conventionally dated **1985–1991**. The program's components: economic liberalization (limited cooperative enterprise, eventually full privatization), political reform (multi-candidate elections within the CPSU, eventual multi-party legalization), foreign-policy reorientation (end of the Brezhnev Doctrine, withdrawal from Afghanistan, German reunification), and cultural opening through [[Glasnost]]. Perestroika ends with the dissolution of the USSR on 26 December 1991.

## Key context

Perestroika's cinema register is enabled by [[Glasnost]] and produces [[Chernukha]] as its dominant style. The lifting of censorship permits the release of previously shelved films ([[11 Brief Encounters|Brief Encounters]] 1968 → released 1986; *Commissar* 1967 → released 1987) and the production of new films depicting Soviet life's actual conditions: alcoholism, family violence, sexual exhaustion, broken communist promises. Perestroika cinema is the formal opposite of [[Modal Schizophrenia]]: where Stalinist SR depicted Soviet life "as it will be," perestroika cinema depicts it "as it actually is — and worse than the official discourse admitted."

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

*Little Vera* (1987) is unimaginable outside perestroika. The film's setting (a Soviet industrial port town, Zhdanov / Mariupol), its content (alcoholism, family violence, explicit sex, communal apartment claustrophobia), and its narrative refusal of redemption are all formally impermissible under late Stalinism, the Thaw, or the Brezhnev era. The film also marks Soviet cinema's first explicit sex scene — the [[Sexual Desublimation]] (Igor Kon) of late-Soviet culture made representable. Vera and her father's drunken family disintegration is perestroika's verdict on the system: the [[Magic Tablecloth]] has been pulled away, and chernukha is what was always underneath.
