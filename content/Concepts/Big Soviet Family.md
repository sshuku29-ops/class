# Big Soviet Family / Big Family Myth 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[06 Chapaev]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate. **The prof identifies this as a foundational framework in the essay rubric — name it explicitly.**

## Source

**Katerina Clark**, *The Soviet Novel: History as Ritual* (University of Chicago Press, 1981), Chapter 5 "The Stalinist Myth of the 'Great Family'." Concept extended throughout the book; pp. 114–135 the densest treatment.

## Definition

Clark's "Great Family" or "Big Family" myth is the foundational ideological structure of Stalinist culture: the Soviet state imagined as an extended family with **Stalin as Father, party leaders as elder brothers, ordinary citizens as children**. Clark writes that Soviet socialist realist novels reproduce a "master plot" in which the protagonist (the spontaneous "son") is guided by a mentor (the conscious "father" or "elder brother") toward ideological maturity. The Big Family replaces the horizontal revolutionary brotherhood of the 1920s with a vertical patriarchal hierarchy.

## Key context

The Big Family myth is the cultural-symbolic structure that organizes Soviet socialist realist art from 1934 onward. It is enforced through [[Socialist Realism]] (the 1934 Writers' Congress aesthetic doctrine), articulated through the [[Spontaneity-Consciousness Dialectic]] (the master plot's narrative engine), and made representable through [[Modal Schizophrenia]] (reality "as it will be"). The hierarchical structure, the prof has emphasized, *a priori precluded gender equality and any other equality* — a vertical structure cannot be a horizontal one. The myth survives the Khrushchev Thaw and the Brezhnev Stagnation in mutated forms, and is finally explicitly deconstructed in post-Soviet cinema (Mikhalkov's [[14 Burnt by the Sun]], Zvyagintsev's [[15 The Return]]).

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

In *Chapaev* (1934), Clark's master plot is enacted at the level of the [[Commissar]]. Furmanov, the party political officer, arrives at Chapaev's Civil War unit and gradually educates the spontaneous peasant commander into Bolshevik consciousness — the [[Spontaneity-Consciousness Dialectic]] in narrative form. Behind Furmanov stands the larger Big Family hierarchy: Furmanov is the elder brother; behind him stand the senior party leadership; behind them stands Stalin as Father. Chapaev's transformation from intuitive Civil War talent to ideologically conscious revolutionary is his entry into the Big Family. His death at the Ural River is the master plot's typical sacrificial ending — the son's conscious sacrifice consolidates the Family.
