# Chernukha / Чернуха 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[13 Little Vera]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate. **The prof asks for stylistic and narrative devices defining chernukha plus a specific scene or image.**

## Source

Russian cultural-cinematic style emerging late 1980s under [[Glasnost]]; canonical academic discussion in **Birgit Beumers**, *A History of Russian Cinema* (Berg, 2009) and Beumers' edited volumes on Russian cinema. Term is colloquial Russian, derived from "чёрный" (black).

## Definition

Chernukha (literally "blackness") is the late-Soviet / post-Soviet cinematic style that focuses on grim everyday reality — alcoholism, family violence, sexual abuse, urban decay, broken communist promises, the rotting infrastructure of Soviet life. Where Stalinist [[Socialist Realism]] depicted Soviet life "as it will be" through [[Modal Schizophrenia]] / the [[Magic Tablecloth]], chernukha insists on showing what was actually there underneath: poverty, exhaustion, brutality. Stylistic devices include: working-class industrial settings, communal apartment claustrophobia, alcohol as constant presence, explicit content (sex, violence, language), refusal of narrative redemption, naturalistic acting, washed-out or grimy mise-en-scène.

## Key context

Chernukha is the dominant style of late-1980s and 1990s post-Soviet cinema — Pichul's *Little Vera* (1987) is foundational, but the wave includes Vasily Pichul, Pyotr Todorovsky, and many others. The style is the formal opposite of Stalinist SR and is enabled politically by [[Glasnost]]. Notably, **Nikita Mikhalkov pointedly OPPOSES chernukha**: his *Burnt by the Sun* (1994) is anti-chernukha in its affirming, golden-hour lighting and pre-Revolutionary intelligentsia nostalgia. The chernukha-vs-Mikhalkov split defines post-Soviet cinema's two main aesthetic camps in the 1990s.

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

In *Little Vera* (1987), the chernukha aesthetic is concentrated in the family-dinner-table scenes: Vera's drunken father and her boyfriend Sergei eat in the cramped apartment, the wallpaper is peeling, the lighting is harsh fluorescent, alcohol bottles dominate the table, the dialogue is hostile and slurred. The eventual stabbing — Vera's father stabs Sergei in a drunken rage — is chernukha's narrative payoff: the system's social failures (alcoholism, family violence, housing crowding) culminate in domestic catastrophe with no redemptive frame. The film's refusal of moral resolution is constitutive: chernukha insists "this is what Soviet life actually was," refusing the [[Magic Tablecloth]] of Stalinist visual tradition.
