# Typage 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[02 Battleship Potemkin]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

**Sergei Eisenstein**, "Through Theatre to Cinema" (1934) and "The Cinematic Principle and the Ideogram" (1929), reprinted in *Film Form: Essays in Film Theory* (ed. Jay Leyda, 1949). Concept developed across Eisenstein's 1920s–30s writings.

## Definition

Typage (типаж) is Eisenstein's principle of casting non-actors based on physical type, not acting skill — selecting faces, bodies, and gestures whose external appearance reads on screen as the social class or ideological category the role represents. The face IS the social position. Eisenstein's intent: "to provide a clear, visually vivid picture of the whole" by replacing individual psychology with collective social typology.

## Key context

Typage is a Marxist response to bourgeois acting tradition. Where Stanislavski's psychological method built characters through individual interiority, Eisenstein's typage builds characters through visible class belonging. Combined with [[Montage of Attractions]] and [[Intellectual Montage]], typage is part of Eisenstein's larger project of replacing the bourgeois individual hero with the [[Mass Hero]].

## Films using it

- 🎯 [[02 Battleship Potemkin]] — the canonical case

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

In *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), Vakulenchuk, the doomed Bolshevik sailor, is played by an actual worker with a weathered, sun-darkened face — typage makes the face itself read as the proletarian condition. In counterpoint, the Tsarist officers are cast for soft features, sneering mouths, and rounded bellies that read on screen as class decadence. Eisenstein writes: "the spectator… should react in the proper way, that is, indignantly, towards the doctor"; the face's visible type does the ideological work that dialogue and psychology do in bourgeois cinema.
