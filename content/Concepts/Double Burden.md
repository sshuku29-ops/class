# Double Burden 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[12 Moscow Doesn't Believe in Tears]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

**Lynne Attwood**, *The New Soviet Man and Woman: Sex-Role Socialization in the USSR* (Indiana University Press, 1990) — primarily Chapter 4 "Khrushchev and the Thaw" and Chapter 5 "Brezhnev: Era of Stagnation." The phrase appears throughout late-Soviet sociology of gender.

## Definition

The Double Burden (двойная нагрузка) names the structural condition of Soviet women's "two virtually full-time jobs" — wage labor outside the home AND domestic labor inside it. Soviet women were declared formally equal to men in employment (universal female workforce participation) without any redistribution of housework, childcare, or food procurement. The result was a chronic time-poverty crisis specific to Soviet women that no Soviet leader formally resolved. **Khrushchev was the first Soviet leader to publicly acknowledge** the Double Burden; **Brezhnev's response was to retreat from women's full-employment expectation** rather than to redistribute domestic labor — the [[Pro-family Campaign]] and [[Equal but Different]] are this retreat.

## Key context

The Double Burden is the central feminist diagnosis of Soviet women's condition. Attwood's larger argument: Soviet ideological commitment to women's emancipation (formal equality, full employment, [[1955 Abortion Re-legalized]]) coexisted with the failure to socialize domestic labor (state cafeterias, communal laundry, public childcare) at scale. The result was a structural double shift that worsened as Stalinist housing crowding eased and women became responsible for individual nuclear-family households. The [[Brezhnev Stagnation]] codified the Double Burden as gender common sense; Moscow's Katerina is its representative figure.

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

In *Moscow Doesn't Believe in Tears* (1979), Katerina runs a factory by day — she is a senior industrial manager — and cooks, cleans, and raises her teenage daughter Aleksandra alone by night, an exemplary instance of Attwood's Double Burden. The film's narrative "solution" is Gosha's arrival as a romantic partner; but Gosha's solution is for Katerina to put her work in second place to her family role — Attwood's diagnosis: "right and proper for the man to be the head of the family." The film resolves the Double Burden not by redistributing labor but by [[Equal but Different]]: Katerina submits her professional success to traditional family hierarchy, and the audience laughs and cheers (Zorkaya, in Attwood). The Double Burden is not solved; it is renamed as love.
