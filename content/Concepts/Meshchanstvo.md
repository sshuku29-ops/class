# Meshchanstvo / Мещанство 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[05 Bed and Sofa]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

Russian cultural-political concept dating to the 19th century; weaponized as a Bolshevik critical category in the 1920s. Discussed in the Bed and Sofa lecture and in **Olga Matich, "The Symbolist Meaning of Love: Theory and Practice"** and broader scholarship on revolutionary byt (everyday life).

## Definition

Meshchanstvo (мещанство) is Russian for "petty-bourgeois philistinism" — the cramped, materialistic, sexually conventional, possession-oriented form of everyday life that Bolsheviks identified as the cultural enemy of revolutionary consciousness. The term derives from "meshchanin" (мещанин), the legal category of small-town tradesman in Imperial Russia, and acquired a secondary moral meaning of cultural narrowness, conformism, and bourgeois domestic preoccupation. Lenin and Trotsky both wrote against meshchanstvo as the cultural form most threatening to revolutionary transformation.

## Key context

For Bolshevik thinkers, meshchanstvo named the danger that revolutionary politics could be undone not by counter-revolution but by the persistence of bourgeois domestic forms — possession, family hierarchy, sexual conventionality — beneath revolutionary rhetoric. NEP (1921–28) was felt to be especially conducive to meshchanstvo because it permitted private trade and small business, allowing petty-bourgeois forms to re-emerge inside the revolutionary state. The party's anxiety about [[NEP]] — the "loss of revolutionary dynamism" and "bourgeois degeneracy" — is the political context for this concept.

## Films using it

- 🎯 [[05 Bed and Sofa]] (1926/27) — the foundational anti-meshchanstvo film

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

The Russian title of *Bed and Sofa* (1926/27) is *Tret'ia Meshchanskaia* — literally "Third Petty-Bourgeois Street," but punning explicitly on meshchanstvo as a moral disease. Abram Room sets the film in a single cramped Moscow apartment shared by Kolia (a construction worker), his wife Liuda, and Kolia's friend Volodia (a printer); the housing crisis forces the ménage à trois that becomes the film's central situation. The two men, despite their proletarian credentials, sink into possessive, complacent, hierarchical domestic patterns — meshchanstvo concentrated. Liuda's escape from the apartment at the film's end (refusing both men, choosing single motherhood) is Room's anti-meshchanstvo verdict: revolutionary gender liberation cannot be achieved without breaking petty-bourgeois domestic form.
