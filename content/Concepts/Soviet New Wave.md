# Soviet New Wave 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[09 The Cranes Are Flying]] on the prof's exam study sheet. The prof says **"see Prokhorov"** — cite him directly.

## Source

**Alexander Prokhorov**, "Springtime for Soviet Cinema: Re/Viewing the 1960s." Term names the Thaw-era cinematic movement; analogous to but distinct from the contemporaneous French New Wave. Concept developed across Prokhorov's writings on Khrushchev-era and Brezhnev-era Soviet film.

## Definition

The Soviet New Wave is Prokhorov's term for [[Khrushchev Thaw]] cinema (roughly 1956–1968), characterized by [[Anti-monumentalism]] (rejection of Stalinist Grand Style), individual subjectivity (replacing the Stalinist mass hero), fragmented narrative (replacing the Socialist Realist [[Master Plot]]), the return of nature and atmospheric mood, the elevation of cinematographers (notably [[Sergei Urusevsky]]) to creative co-authorship, and the entrance of female filmmakers (Larisa Shepit'ko, Kira Muratova) into the canon. The movement is the cinema of "the children of the Twentieth Congress."

## Key context

The Soviet New Wave is the cinematic register of the Thaw's broader cultural opening. Prokhorov's defining moves: the [[Femininity]] of the new Soviet protagonist (Veronika as flawed individual rather than Stakhanovite type); the subjective camera ([[Sergei Urusevsky]] in *Cranes*); [[Associative Linking]] (Tarkovsky's editing principle); [[Characterness]] and [[Narrative Discontinuity]] (Muratova's contributions). The movement is bookended by Khrushchev's Secret Speech (1956) and the Czechoslovakia invasion (1968) — Brief Encounters, shelved 1968, marks the New Wave's terminus.

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

Prokhorov identifies *The Cranes Are Flying* (1957) as the foundational Soviet New Wave film through three formal moves: (1) Sergei Urusevsky's subjective camera, inheriting the Constructivist line through his teacher Aleksandr Rodchenko, replacing the Stalinist objective monumental shot; (2) Veronika as flawed female protagonist, replacing the Stakhanovite mother; (3) the Stalinist family-and-war trope inverted — Boris does not return; Veronika is "unfaithful" (forced by Mark); the Big Family contract is broken. Each move is a refusal of Stalinist monumentalism and an inauguration of New Wave individuality.
