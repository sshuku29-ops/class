# Intellectual Montage / Dialectical Montage 🎯

> **🎯 STUDY SHEET CONCEPT** — listed under [[02 Battleship Potemkin]] on the prof's exam study sheet. Part I-B Option 1 candidate.

## Source

**Sergei Eisenstein**, "The Cinematic Principle and the Ideogram" (1929), reprinted in *Film Form*. Concept developed across "A Dialectic Approach to Film Form" (1929) and "The Filmic Fourth Dimension" (1929).

## Definition

Intellectual montage (also called dialectical montage) is editing as Marxist dialectic: Shot A and Shot B collide to produce Idea C, an abstraction the viewer constructs from the cut itself rather than from either shot alone. Eisenstein's formulation: "from the collision of two given factors arises a concept." Where standard editing serves narrative continuity, intellectual montage serves ideological synthesis.

## Key context

Intellectual montage applies Marx's dialectical method (thesis + antithesis = synthesis) to the cut. It is Eisenstein's attempt to make cinema a thinking medium, not just a representational one. The viewer becomes a participant in the production of meaning rather than a consumer of finished narrative. This is the formal expression of Soviet montage theory's most ambitious claim: that revolutionary cinema can produce abstract political thought through purely visual means.

## Films using it

- 🎯 [[02 Battleship Potemkin]] — the lions sequence

## Concrete example (write this in your exam answer)

In *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), three statues of marble lions at the Vorontsov / Alupka Palace — one sleeping, one waking, one rising — are intercut with the Potemkin's cannons firing on the Tsarist headquarters. The three static statues become one animated lion through editing alone; the synthesized idea is "the masses awaken." Neither shot of any individual lion contains motion; the abstraction (the awakening) is produced entirely by the cut. Eisenstein: "the juxtaposition of two separate shots… resembles not so much their sum as their product."
